Making sales does not automatically mean your business is making a profit.

A shop can have customers every day, sell products regularly, and still have very little money left at the end of the month.

To understand how your business is performing, you need to know the difference between sales, expenses, and profit.

This guide explains how to calculate profit in a small business, what costs to include, common mistakes to avoid, and how better financial records can help you understand what is really happening in your business.

What Is Profit?

Profit is the money your business has left after subtracting the costs of running the business from the money it earned.

A simple way to think about it is:

Profit = Revenue − Expenses

However, for a shop that buys and sells products, calculating profit accurately also requires you to consider the cost of the products that were actually sold.

For example, if your shop sells products worth 500,000 CFA during a period, you cannot automatically say that your profit is 500,000 CFA.

You still have to account for the cost of the products sold and other business expenses.

Sales Are Not the Same as Profit

One of the most common mistakes small business owners make is treating sales as profit.

Imagine a shop sells products worth:

1,000,000 CFA

The products sold originally cost the business:

700,000 CFA

The business also had other expenses of:

100,000 CFA

The calculation would be:

Revenue: 1,000,000 CFA
Cost of products sold: 700,000 CFA
Other expenses: 100,000 CFA

Profit = 1,000,000 − 700,000 − 100,000

Profit = 200,000 CFA

The business made 1,000,000 CFA in sales, but its profit was 200,000 CFA.

That difference is important.

Gross Profit and Net Profit

There are two useful profit measurements that small businesses should understand.

Gross Profit

Gross profit is what remains after subtracting the cost of the products sold from sales revenue.

Gross Profit = Sales Revenue − Cost of Goods Sold

Using the example above:

1,000,000 CFA − 700,000 CFA = 300,000 CFA gross profit

Net Profit

Net profit takes other business expenses into account.

Net Profit = Gross Profit − Operating Expenses

If the business spent another 100,000 CFA on expenses:

300,000 CFA − 100,000 CFA = 200,000 CFA net profit

Net profit gives you a better picture of what the business actually earned after its recorded costs.

Step 1: Record Every Sale

The first step in calculating profit is keeping accurate sales records.

For each sale, your records should ideally show:

  • What was sold
  • Quantity sold
  • Selling price
  • Date of sale
  • Whether the sale was cash or credit
  • Customer information when relevant
  • The employee who handled the sale when applicable

If sales are missing from your records, your revenue information will not be reliable.

This becomes even more important when a business handles both cash and credit sales.

A credit sale is still a sale, even though the customer has not paid the full amount yet.

Step 2: Know the Cost of the Products You Sell

Knowing your selling price is not enough.

You also need to know what the products cost your business.

For example, if you buy a product for 5,000 CFA and sell it for 7,000 CFA, the difference before other expenses is:

7,000 − 5,000 = 2,000 CFA

That 2,000 CFA is the product's gross margin in this simple example.

If you do not know your product costs, it becomes difficult to determine whether your prices are generating enough margin.

Step 3: Track Business Expenses

Sales and product costs are not the only numbers that matter.

A business may have expenses such as:

  • Rent
  • Electricity
  • Water
  • Transport
  • Salaries
  • Packaging
  • Repairs
  • Communication
  • Bank or payment charges
  • Other operating costs

Keeping these expenses separate and recording them consistently makes it easier to understand where your money is going.

Step 4: Calculate Your Gross Profit

Once you know your sales and the cost of the products sold, calculate gross profit.

Gross Profit = Sales Revenue − Cost of Goods Sold

For example:

Sales = 2,000,000 CFA

Cost of products sold = 1,400,000 CFA

Gross profit = 600,000 CFA

This tells you how much remains from your sales before other operating expenses are deducted.

Step 5: Calculate Your Net Profit

Next, subtract your other recorded business expenses.

Suppose your gross profit is:

600,000 CFA

And your operating expenses are:

250,000 CFA

Then:

Net Profit = 600,000 − 250,000

Net Profit = 350,000 CFA

This gives you a clearer picture of the profit generated during that period based on the records you have entered.

What About Stock That Has Not Been Sold?

This is an important point for businesses that keep inventory.

If you buy 1,000,000 CFA worth of products but only sell 600,000 CFA worth of those products during the period, you should not automatically treat the entire 1,000,000 CFA purchase as the cost of products sold for that period.

Some of the products may still be sitting in your inventory.

This is why good stock records are closely connected to good profit information.

You need to understand:

  • What you purchased
  • What you sold
  • What remains in stock
  • The cost associated with the products sold

Accurate inventory records help make this information more useful.

Why Credit Sales Can Make Profit Difficult to Understand

Credit sales create another challenge.

Suppose you sell products worth 200,000 CFA to a customer on credit.

The sale has happened, but you may not have received the 200,000 CFA in cash yet.

Your business therefore needs to keep track of both:

Sales: 200,000 CFA

and

Amount still owed: 200,000 CFA

If the customer later makes a partial payment of 80,000 CFA, the remaining debt becomes:

200,000 − 80,000 = 120,000 CFA

This is why sales records, customer debt records, repayments, and cash information should not be confused with one another.

Common Profit Calculation Mistakes

1. Treating all sales as profit

Sales are revenue, not automatically profit.

2. Forgetting business expenses

Rent, transport, salaries, utilities, and other costs can reduce the money left after sales.

3. Ignoring product costs

If you do not know what your products cost, you cannot properly understand your gross profit.

4. Counting stock purchases as if everything was sold

Products that remain in inventory have not necessarily become part of the cost of products sold for the period.

5. Ignoring credit sales

Credit sales still need to be recorded even when the cash has not yet been collected.

6. Mixing personal and business spending

When personal spending is mixed with business expenses, it becomes harder to understand how the business itself is performing.

7. Relying entirely on memory

When sales, expenses, purchases, debts, and repayments are written down inconsistently—or remembered later—important information can be missed.

How Often Should You Calculate Profit?

There is no single schedule that works for every business.

However, regularly reviewing your numbers can help you identify problems earlier.

You might review your business:

  • Daily for sales and cash activity
  • Weekly for stock and outstanding debts
  • Monthly for revenue, expenses, and profit

The important thing is consistency.

Regular reviews can help you notice changes in sales, expenses, stock movement, and customer debts before they become larger problems.

What Information Do You Need to Calculate Profit?

At a minimum, you need reliable records of:

  • Sales revenue
  • Product costs
  • Business expenses
  • Stock purchases
  • Inventory remaining
  • Credit sales
  • Customer repayments

The more organized these records are, the easier it becomes to understand your business.

A Simple Monthly Profit Review

At the end of each month, you can review your business using a simple process:

  1. Add up your sales. Include both cash and credit sales recorded during the period.
  2. Determine the cost of the products sold. Use your inventory and purchase records to understand the cost associated with the products sold.
  3. Calculate gross profit. Sales − Cost of Products Sold = Gross Profit
  4. Add your recorded operating expenses. Include the relevant expenses for the period.
  5. Calculate net profit. Gross Profit − Operating Expenses = Net Profit
  6. Review what changed. Compare your results with previous periods and look for meaningful changes in sales, expenses, stock, or customer debts.

Profit Margin: Another Useful Number

Profit can also be expressed as a percentage of revenue.

A simple net profit margin calculation is:

Net Profit Margin = (Net Profit ÷ Revenue) × 100

For example, if a business has:

Revenue = 1,000,000 CFA

Net profit = 200,000 CFA

Then:

(200,000 ÷ 1,000,000) × 100 = 20%

The net profit margin is 20%.

This percentage can help you compare business performance across different periods, provided the underlying records are consistent.

Final Takeaway

Knowing your sales is useful, but knowing your profit is even more important.

A business owner should be able to answer questions such as:

  • How much did we sell?
  • How much did the products cost?
  • How much did we spend?
  • How much are customers still owing?
  • What stock do we still have?
  • How much profit did the business generate?

These questions become much easier to answer when sales, stock, expenses, customer debts, and repayments are recorded consistently.

Good financial records turn business activity into information you can actually use.
